THE SHAPE OF WAITING

An essay on queues, scarcity, and the systems we build to decide who goes first.


I. THE UNIVERSAL EXPERIENCE

Every system that shares something scarce eventually invents a queue, and every queue eventually invents a politics. This is true of the single checkout lane at a corner shop, of the emergency room at two in the morning, of the merge point where four lanes of traffic become two, and of the processor that has more work than cores. The particulars differ enormously. The structure does not. Somewhere there is a resource that cannot serve everyone at once, somewhere there is a population that wants it, and somewhere in between there is an ordering, explicit or implicit, deliberate or accidental, that decides whose turn comes next.

We tend to think of waiting as a failure. The queue at the bank is a sign that the bank is understaffed. The spinning cursor is a sign that the software is badly written. The traffic jam is a sign that the road was poorly planned. Sometimes this is true. More often it is a misunderstanding of what a queue is for. A queue is not primarily a symptom of insufficiency. It is a buffer that lets a system with variable demand use a resource with fixed capacity at a high rate of occupancy. Without a queue, every arrival that finds the resource busy would simply be turned away, and the resource would sit idle every time demand dipped. The queue is what converts a bursty, unpredictable arrival process into a smooth, predictable service process. It is a shock absorber made of time.

This reframing matters because it changes the question. The question is not "how do we eliminate waiting," which usually means "how do we buy so much capacity that demand never exceeds it," an answer that is either unaffordable or wasteful and frequently both. The question is "what shape should the waiting take." Who waits, how long, with what certainty, and with what recourse. Those are design decisions, and they are made whether or not anyone makes them consciously. A system with no stated policy still has a policy. It is simply one that emerged from implementation details rather than from intent.

Consider the humble supermarket. A row of checkout lanes, each with its own line, is a different machine from a single serpentine line feeding whichever register opens next. The two arrangements have identical throughput. The same cashiers process the same shoppers at the same rate. What differs is the distribution of the wait. In the row of lanes, you choose a line and live with your choice. If the person in front of you has a price check, a coupon dispute, and a checkbook, you lose, while the person who chose the neighboring lane wins. The average wait across all shoppers is the same, but the variance is enormous, and the experience is one of luck and regret. The serpentine line abolishes the lottery. Everyone waits about the same amount, which is the average, and no one watches a latecomer in the next lane finish first. The average is unchanged. The felt experience is transformed.

That is the first lesson of queues, and the one most often missed: the average tells you almost nothing about how the waiting feels. Two systems with identical mean latency can be experienced as night and day, depending on the spread. A system where everyone waits four minutes is not the same as a system where three quarters of people wait one minute and the rest wait thirteen. The second has a better story to tell in the quarterly report and a worse story to tell at the customer service desk. Most complaints come from the tail, and the tail is invisible to the mean.


II. THE ARITHMETIC OF CONTENTION

There is a piece of arithmetic that anyone who builds shared systems should carry around, and it is this: waiting does not grow in proportion to load. It grows faster, and near the top of the range it grows without bound.

The intuition is easy to state. Imagine a server that takes exactly one second to handle each request, and imagine requests arriving exactly one second apart, like a metronome. Utilization is total, one hundred percent, and yet no one ever waits, because each arrival finds the server just finishing the last job. This is the fantasy of perfect scheduling, and it exists nowhere outside of textbooks, because real arrivals do not come from a metronome. They come in clusters. Three arrive in the same second, then none for four seconds, then two more. The long run average may still be one per second, but the clusters create backlogs that the quiet stretches must drain, and the drain takes time.

Now raise the arrival rate slightly. The quiet stretches get shorter. The backlogs from each cluster have less time to clear before the next cluster lands. At some point the backlog from one burst has not cleared when the next burst begins, and the queue begins to accumulate across bursts rather than within them. Past that point, small increases in load produce large increases in wait, and the relationship becomes vertical: the last few percent of utilization cost more waiting than all the preceding percent combined.

This is why systems that look comfortable at seventy percent utilization fall apart at ninety, and why the operator who says "we have thirty percent headroom" is often describing a cliff rather than a cushion. It is also why variability is the enemy. Two systems can have the same mean service time and the same mean arrival rate and behave completely differently, because one has consistent service times and the other has a mix of quick jobs and enormous ones. The enormous ones do not merely take longer themselves. They block everything behind them, and the blocking compounds.

Anyone who has waited behind a single shopper with a full trolley while holding a carton of milk has felt this in their bones. The trolley shopper is not being unreasonable. They are simply a high-variance job in a system that did not plan for variance. The express lane exists precisely to segregate variance, to keep the small jobs away from the large ones so that the small jobs do not inherit the large ones' delays. Nearly every serious queueing system eventually reinvents the express lane in some form.

The second arithmetic fact is subtler and more useful: for a given amount of work, the way you order the jobs changes the total waiting, even though it cannot change the total working. Suppose four jobs arrive at the same instant, taking one, one, one, and ten units of time. If you run the long one first, the three short jobs finish at eleven, twelve, and thirteen, and the average completion time across all four is about eleven and a half. If you run the short ones first, they finish at one, two, and three, the long one finishes at thirteen, and the average is about four and three quarters. The machine did exactly the same amount of work in exactly the same total time. The only thing that changed was the order, and the average wait fell by more than half.

This is the strongest argument in favor of running short jobs first, and it is mathematically airtight: if you want to minimize average completion time for a fixed set of jobs, run them in order of increasing size. It is also, taken alone, a recipe for cruelty. A policy that always prefers the short job is a policy under which the long job may never run at all, because in any busy system there is always another short job arriving. The long job sits there, perpetually postponed, while the system reports excellent average latency and the owner of the long job slowly loses their mind.

We have arrived, by arithmetic, at the central tension of every scheduler ever built. Efficiency and justice pull in opposite directions. The ordering that makes the numbers look best is the ordering that treats some participant worst. Every real system resolves this tension somehow, usually with a compromise that no one finds elegant and everyone can live with.


III. WHO GOES FIRST

The simplest policy is the oldest: first come, first served. It has the great virtue of being obviously defensible. Nobody argues with the person who was there before them, or at least nobody argues successfully. It requires no knowledge of the jobs, no estimate of their size, no classification of their importance. It is the policy of the bakery ticket dispenser and the airport check-in line, and its legitimacy comes from the fact that arrival order is a thing everybody can observe and nobody can dispute.

Its weakness is the trolley shopper. First come, first served is maximally vulnerable to variance, because one enormous job at the head of the line delays everything behind it by its full duration. In computing this failure has a name, head of line blocking, and it is the reason that a single slow request can make an entire service look broken. The queue behind it is not slow. It is stuck.

The opposite policy is strict priority: rank the jobs by some notion of importance and always serve the highest rank available. Hospitals do this and call it triage, and in that setting it is not merely defensible but morally obligatory. The person with the severed artery goes before the person with the sprained ankle, regardless of who arrived first, because the consequences of waiting are wildly different for the two of them. Strict priority is the right answer whenever the cost of delay differs enough between classes that arrival order becomes morally irrelevant.

But strict priority has the same disease as shortest job first, in a more acute form. If high priority work keeps arriving, low priority work never runs. Starvation is not a hypothetical. It is the default outcome of strict priority under sustained load, and every system that uses priority has to bolt on a defense against it. The usual defense is aging: a job's effective priority rises the longer it waits, so that a sufficiently patient low priority job eventually outranks a freshly arrived high priority one. Aging is an admission that pure priority is untenable, a confession dressed as a feature. It works, and it introduces a new parameter that someone has to tune, and the tuning is always a judgment call about how much injustice is tolerable and for how long.

Between these poles live the round robin policies, which give each participant a slice of the resource in rotation. Round robin has a property the others lack: it is indifferent to job size in the short term. A participant with an enormous job does not block anyone, because their slice ends and the next participant gets theirs. The enormous job simply takes many slices to complete. This is why interactive systems love round robin. It guarantees that the responsiveness of a small job does not depend on what else is running, which is exactly what a person sitting at a keyboard needs.

The cost of round robin is switching. Every rotation has an overhead, and if the slice is small relative to the overhead, the system spends its time changing its mind rather than doing work. This is the third fundamental tension, after efficiency against justice and average against tail: responsiveness against overhead. Fine slices give snappy interaction and waste capacity. Coarse slices use the resource efficiently and make everything feel sluggish. The correct slice size is an empirical question whose answer changes whenever the hardware changes, which is why the number is always in a configuration file and never in a proof.


IV. THE COST OF CHANGING YOUR MIND

There is a hidden expense in every scheduling decision, and it is the cost of the switch itself. Moving from one job to another is never free. The clerk who was halfway through your paperwork and now turns to someone else has to remember where they were. The surgeon who leaves one operating theater for another must scrub again. The processor that suspends one task and resumes another must save and restore a pile of state, and, far more expensively, must watch its caches fill with the wrong data and slowly refill with the right data.

This last cost is the one that surprises people, because it is invisible in the accounting. The explicit switch, the saving of registers and the loading of a new address space, is measured in microseconds and is genuinely small. The implicit switch, the loss of everything the machine had learned about the work it was doing, is measured in the thousands of cycles that the new job spends waiting for memory that would have been resident if the old job had not evicted it. A scheduler that ignores this cost will happily produce an ordering that looks optimal on paper and runs at half speed in practice.

The same effect appears at every scale. A factory line that switches between products pays a changeover cost, and the economics of batch size in manufacturing are almost exactly the economics of time slice length in a scheduler. Make the batches too small and you spend your life reconfiguring the line. Make them too large and every order waits for the batch it belongs to. A person switching between tasks pays the same tax in the currency of attention, and the recovery time after an interruption is famously longer than the interruption itself.

The practical consequence is that affinity is valuable. Keeping related work together, on the same machine, in the same batch, under the same context, pays for itself in ways that a naive model of the system will not predict. The corollary is that a scheduler which knows nothing about affinity will make decisions that are locally sensible and globally expensive. It will move a job to an idle resource because idle resources should be used, and in doing so it will throw away all the accumulated state that made the job fast where it was.

This creates a genuine dilemma, not a puzzle with a clean answer. Should a job wait for the resource that already knows about it, or run immediately on a resource that does not? The answer depends on quantities the scheduler usually cannot measure: how much state there is, how expensive rebuilding it would be, how long the preferred resource will stay busy. Systems that guess well at this look magical. Systems that guess badly thrash, which is to say they spend so much of their capacity on switching that very little is left for the work.

Thrashing deserves a paragraph of its own, because it is the characteristic failure mode of an overloaded sharing system and because it is qualitatively different from mere slowness. A slow system does less work per unit time. A thrashing system does less work per unit time as load increases, which means that adding demand reduces output. The feedback is positive in the mathematical sense and catastrophic in the practical one. Every participant, seeing poor service, tries harder, retries more, asks for more, and their collective effort to get service is precisely what prevents anyone from getting it. The queue at the door grows because the door is jammed by the crowd.

The defenses against thrashing are all forms of saying no. Admission control, which refuses new work at the gate rather than accepting it and failing to serve it. Backpressure, which propagates the refusal upstream so that the producers slow down instead of piling on. Load shedding, which drops some work deliberately so that the rest can be served properly. All three feel like failures to the people implementing them, because all three involve turning away demand that the system in principle wanted. All three are what separates a system that degrades gracefully from one that collapses.


V. WHY SYSTEMS REMEMBER

Nearly every serious queueing system eventually grows a memory, and the memory changes the scheduling problem in a way that most treatments ignore.

The memory usually arrives as a cache. Having done some expensive preparation for one participant, the system keeps the result, on the theory that the same participant will be back. This is obviously sensible. It is also the moment when jobs stop being interchangeable. Before the cache, any resource could serve any job equally well, and the scheduler's only concern was ordering. After the cache, the resources are no longer identical, because each one remembers a different subset of the work, and sending a job to the resource that remembers it is dramatically cheaper than sending it elsewhere.

This transforms scheduling from a question of order into a question of placement, and placement problems are harder. Order is one dimensional. Placement is a matching between jobs and resources, with a cost matrix that changes every time a job runs, because running a job changes what its resource remembers. The scheduler is now playing a game against its own future self, and greedy choices that look cheap now can be expensive later.

The memory also has a capacity, which means it has an eviction policy, which means it has all the same politics as the queue itself, one level down. Which memory do we keep when we cannot keep everything? The honest answer in most systems is: whatever was used most recently, on the assumption that recency predicts reuse. That assumption is usually right and occasionally catastrophically wrong. A single sweep through a large body of work, touching everything once and returning to nothing, will evict everything valuable and replace it with things that will never be used again. The cache does not merely fail to help during such a sweep. It actively hurts, because the effort of maintaining it is wasted and the useful contents are gone when normal work resumes.

Systems that care about this segregate their memory, giving scanning work its own small region so that it cannot wipe out the region serving interactive work. This is the express lane again, in a different costume. The pattern recurs so often that it deserves to be stated as a principle: when a shared resource serves populations with different access patterns, the populations will interfere destructively unless they are separated, and the separation must be built deliberately because it will not emerge on its own.

There is a further subtlety in the interaction between memory and ordering. If the scheduler prefers whichever job the memory already serves, it will produce excellent throughput and a starvation problem, because a steady stream of jobs that hit the cache will indefinitely postpone the job that would require the cache to be replaced. This is shortest job first wearing yet another costume: the cached job is the cheap job, the uncached job is the expensive one, and preferring cheap work forever means expensive work never happens. The remedy is the same, an aging term that eventually forces the expensive switch, and the tuning of that term is the same judgment call about tolerable injustice.

What makes this version harder is that the cost of the switch is not fixed. It depends on what is in the memory, how large the state is, and whether the displaced participant will return soon. A scheduler that always pays the switching cost is wasteful. A scheduler that never pays it is unjust. And no fixed threshold is correct for all workloads, which is why these parameters end up in configuration files with comments that read like apologies.


VI. THE QUEUE AS A SOCIAL CONTRACT

So far this has been an essay about machines. But queues existed long before computers, and the human versions have solved, or failed to solve, exactly the same problems, with the added complication that the participants can see each other and have opinions.

The physical queue, the line of people, is one of the most remarkable pieces of social technology ever invented. It has no enforcement mechanism beyond disapproval, no central authority, and no record keeping, and yet in most places it works. Its legitimacy rests entirely on a shared understanding of arrival order and a collective willingness to punish violations with nothing more than glares. The person who cuts in line is not arrested. They are simply made to feel the weight of several dozen silent judgments, and for most people that is enough.

This legitimacy is fragile and depends on visibility. People accept waiting when they can see that the queue is moving and that the people ahead of them are being served in order. They become furious when the order is opaque, not because the wait is longer but because they cannot verify that they are being treated the same as everyone else. A hidden queue is experienced as an unjust one even when it is perfectly ordered, which is why the single most effective intervention in many waiting systems is not to reduce the wait but to display it. The estimated time of arrival, the ticket number on the wall, the progress bar: none of these make the work go faster, and all of them make the waiting tolerable, because they convert an indefinite wait into a definite one.

The corollary is that a progress indicator which lies is worse than none at all. Trust, once spent, is not easily recovered, and a system that has told you it is nearly done three times will not be believed on the fourth. Queue design is partly the design of promises, and promises that cannot be kept should not be made.

Human queues also demonstrate the limits of pure arrival order. Every society that has thought about it has decided that some people should go first regardless of when they arrived: the person in labor, the person bleeding, sometimes the elderly, sometimes the person with one item. These exceptions are never derived from a theory. They are negotiated, contested, and revised, and the negotiation is a moral argument rather than a technical one. What is instructive for the engineer is that the exceptions are always explicit and always few. A queue with one well understood exception remains legitimate. A queue with a dozen opaque exceptions is no longer a queue. It is a discretionary allocation that has kept the visual appearance of a line, and people can tell the difference immediately.

There is a final human lesson, about what happens when the wait becomes unbearable. People do not wait indefinitely. They leave, and they either go elsewhere or come back later, and in both cases the system's picture of demand becomes wrong. Abandonment is a form of silent load shedding, performed by the customers rather than the operator, and a system which does not measure it will systematically underestimate the demand it is failing to serve. The queue that looks short may be short because everyone gave up.


VII. THE TYRANNY OF THE TAIL

If there is one idea from the study of queues that deserves to escape into general circulation, it is this: in any system made of many parts, the behavior of the whole is governed by the worst behavior of the parts, not the typical one.

The argument is simple and its consequences are severe. Suppose a request to your system requires touching ten components, each of which is fast almost always and slow one time in a hundred. Each component, considered alone, looks excellent: ninety nine percent of its responses are quick. But a request touches all ten, and it is slow if any one of them is slow. The chance that all ten are quick is ninety nine percent raised to the tenth power, which is about ninety percent. One request in ten is now slow, in a system where every single component is fast ninety nine times out of a hundred. Add more components, or make the requests fan out more widely, and the arithmetic becomes brutal. A service that touches a hundred components and wants a good experience for the great majority of its users needs each component to be slow less than one time in ten thousand.

This is why the tail matters so much more than the average in any system built by composition. Improving the median does almost nothing for the composed experience. Improving the ninety ninth percentile does almost everything. And the tail is where all the unglamorous problems live: the occasional lock contention, the periodic maintenance pause, the retry after a transient failure, the one request that arrived just as a cache was being replaced.

The tail is also where queueing effects concentrate. A queue with light load has a tail dominated by the service time itself. A queue with heavy load has a tail dominated by waiting, and the waiting is heavy precisely when several large jobs happen to cluster. Since clustering is a statistical certainty given enough time, the tail is not an anomaly to be debugged away. It is a structural property of the arrangement, and it can be moved but not abolished.

There are three broad responses to tail latency, and each is a trade.

The first is to reduce variance at the source, by making the slow cases less slow. Break the enormous job into pieces so that it no longer blocks. Bound the work any single request can demand. Put a ceiling on retries. This is the most durable fix and usually the most invasive, because it means changing the work rather than the scheduling of it.

The second is to hide variance with redundancy: send the request to two places and take whichever answers first. This works startlingly well when the slowness is uncorrelated and independent, because the probability that both copies are slow is the product of two small numbers. It also doubles the load, which raises utilization, which by the arithmetic of contention makes everything slower, and so the technique only pays when there is slack. Used carelessly on a saturated system, hedging is an accelerant rather than an extinguisher.

The third is to admit defeat selectively: set a deadline, and when it expires, return whatever you have. A partial answer delivered on time is frequently more useful than a complete answer delivered late, and the decision about which is a product question rather than an engineering one. Systems that can degrade in this way are far more robust than systems that must either succeed completely or fail completely, but building them requires that every layer be able to produce a meaningful partial result, which is a demand most architectures cannot meet without being designed for it from the start.

What unites all three responses is that they treat the tail as a first class design concern rather than as a bug to be fixed later. Retrofitting tail tolerance onto a system that assumed everything would be fast is among the least rewarding activities in engineering.


VIII. MEASURING THE RIGHT THING

None of this can be managed without measurement, and measurement of queueing systems is full of traps.

The first trap is measuring only service time and calling it latency. The time your component spends working on a request is not the time the requester experienced. The requester experienced the waiting as well, and in a loaded system the waiting dominates. A dashboard showing that the median service time is unchanged while users complain bitterly is a dashboard measuring the wrong end of the queue. The number that matters is measured from the moment the request was made, not from the moment it was picked up.

The second trap is averaging across populations that should not be averaged. If half the traffic is tiny requests and half is enormous ones, the mean latency describes nobody's experience. It is the temperature of a room with one end on fire and the other in a freezer. Segment first, then measure, and be suspicious of any single number that summarizes a mixture.

The third trap is the coordinated omission that occurs when the measurement harness itself waits. If your load generator sends a request, waits for the response, and only then sends the next, then whenever the system is slow the generator automatically reduces its offered load. The system's worst behavior is hidden, because the measurement stopped pushing exactly when pushing would have revealed the problem. The result is a benchmark that looks better the worse the system behaves. The correct approach is to send requests on a schedule and record the delay against the schedule, so that a slow system produces a growing backlog in the measurement rather than a tactful pause.

The fourth trap is measuring the queue's length rather than its age. Length is easy to instrument and tells you less than you think, because a long queue that drains quickly is healthy and a short queue that has not moved in a minute is a catastrophe. The age of the oldest waiting item is the number that predicts trouble, and it is the number that tells you whether your anti starvation mechanisms are doing their job.

The fifth trap, and the most human, is that measurements shape behavior. Once a number is reported, it becomes a target, and once it is a target it will be optimized, including in ways that damage everything the number failed to capture. A team measured on average latency will discover that abandoning slow requests improves the average. A team measured on throughput will discover that batching everything improves throughput at the cost of every individual's experience. The metric is never the goal. It is a proxy for the goal, and the gap between the proxy and the goal is where all the perverse incentives live.


IX. WHEN THE RESOURCE MUST BE SHARED

There is a special and difficult case that deserves separate treatment: the resource that cannot be subdivided but can be reassigned, where reassignment is expensive and the participants have long memories.

A surgical theater is like this. A printing press with an expensive plate change is like this. So is any computing resource whose usefulness depends on a large body of loaded state, where serving a different population means discarding that state and loading another.

Such resources produce a scheduling problem with a particular flavor. The switching cost means the scheduler wants long runs: serve one population for a while, then another, rather than alternating rapidly. The participants, meanwhile, want short waits, and short waits require rapid alternation. The optimum is a compromise on run length, and the compromise depends on the ratio between the switching cost and the tolerable wait, a ratio that can differ by orders of magnitude between workloads that look superficially similar.

Two failure modes bracket the compromise. If the runs are too short, the resource spends most of its time switching and very little serving, and everybody waits longer than they would under any sane policy. If the runs are too long, the resource is efficient and some population is effectively excluded for the duration, which from that population's perspective is indistinguishable from the resource not existing. The second failure is more insidious because the system's own metrics look excellent throughout. Utilization is high, throughput is high, and the mean latency, computed over the requests that were actually served, is fine. The excluded population's experience simply does not appear in the numbers, because their requests are still waiting and unfinished requests contribute to no average.

This is the deepest reason to measure the age of the oldest waiting item rather than the latency of the completed ones. Completed requests are a biased sample. They are the ones the system chose to serve, and if the system is systematically choosing against some class of work, that class will be invisible in every statistic derived from completions. The queue's oldest member is the only witness to the policy's injustice, and a system that does not watch that number is a system that has arranged not to know.

The practical resolution in most such systems is a hybrid. Prefer the population already in residence, because switching is expensive and preference is efficient, but maintain a clock on everybody else, and when that clock expires, switch regardless of efficiency. The clock's duration is the explicit statement of how much injustice the operator will tolerate in exchange for how much throughput. Making that number explicit, visible, and configurable is the difference between a policy and an accident.


X. THE ILLUSION OF PARALLELISM

When a queue grows, the reflex is to add servers, and the reflex is usually right. But the relationship between servers and waiting is not the simple proportion that intuition suggests, and the ways it departs from that proportion are instructive.

Start with the good news, which is better than intuition expects. A single server at high utilization has a terrible queue, and two servers sharing the same total capacity, fed from a single line, have a much better one. This is not merely because capacity doubled. It is because pooling reduces the probability that all servers are simultaneously busy with long jobs, and that probability is what drives the waiting. Two half speed servers fed by one line beat one full speed server for the same reason the serpentine checkout beats the row of lanes: the pool absorbs variance that individual assignment cannot.

Now the bad news. This benefit requires a single shared line. The moment you split the line, assigning each arrival to a server on arrival, you throw the pooling benefit away and get the lottery back. Some server will be idle while another has three jobs waiting, and no amount of adding servers repairs the structural error of premature assignment. In distributed systems this mistake is made constantly, usually by a load balancer that assigns a request to a backend at arrival time, on the basis of a hash or a rotation, and then cannot change its mind. The fix is always to delay the binding: let the request wait in a shared place, and let whichever server becomes free take the next one.

Where binding cannot be delayed, the next best thing is to make the assignment on the basis of current occupancy rather than history. Choosing the least loaded of two randomly sampled servers is famously effective, far more effective than choosing one at random and almost as good as choosing the least loaded of all of them, at a tiny fraction of the coordination cost. The lesson generalizes: a small amount of information about the present state beats an enormous amount of clever reasoning about averages.

The harder limit on parallelism is that not all of the work divides. Every task has a part that must be done in sequence, and no amount of additional capacity compresses that part. If a tenth of the work is inherently serial, then ten servers do not give you a tenfold speedup, they give you something closer to fivefold, and a hundred servers give you not much more than that. The serial fraction sets a ceiling, and the ceiling is reached faster than anyone wants.

Worse, the serial fraction is rarely where you expect. It is not usually a stretch of computation. It is a lock, a shared counter, a single coordinator that every participant must consult, a log that all writes must pass through. Such things are invisible in a functional description of the system and dominant in its performance. The discipline of finding them consists largely of looking for the places where everyone must agree, because agreement is the enemy of parallelism, and every point of agreement is a queue that did not appear in the design.

There is also a cost to parallelism that grows rather than shrinking with scale: the coordination overhead itself. Every additional participant must be tracked, informed, and synchronized, and at some point the effort of managing the parallelism exceeds the benefit of having it. Every large system has a point past which adding capacity makes it slower, and the fact that this point exists surprises people every time they meet it. A system at that point exhibits a signature that is easy to recognize once seen: throughput rises with load, plateaus, and then declines, while latency rises continuously. The decline is the tell. Capacity is being consumed by the machinery of sharing rather than by the work.


XI. DESIGNING A QUEUE YOU CAN DEFEND

Pulling the threads together, a set of principles emerges. None is original, and none is unconditional, but together they describe what a defensible queue looks like.

Make the policy explicit. A system that has not decided how it orders work has still decided, by accident, and the accidental policy is almost always worse and always harder to explain. Write down what goes first and why. The act of writing it down will reveal cases nobody had considered.

Separate populations that interfere. Express lanes, priority classes, dedicated pools, segregated caches. Whenever two kinds of work have different sizes, different urgencies, or different access patterns, sharing a single undifferentiated queue between them will produce an outcome bad for both. Separation is the single highest leverage intervention available in most systems.

Bound everything. Bound the queue length, so that unbounded backlogs cannot form. Bound the service time, so that one job cannot occupy the resource forever. Bound the retries, so that failure does not amplify. Bound the memory, so that caching cannot starve the work. Every unbounded quantity in a shared system is a latent outage waiting for the right burst of demand.

Prefer to refuse early rather than accept and disappoint. A request rejected at the gate costs the requester one round trip and leaves them free to try elsewhere. A request accepted and then queued for minutes costs them the wait, the uncertainty, and frequently the timeout, and costs the system the resources of holding it. Honest refusal is a kindness.

Watch the oldest, not the average. The average describes the population you served. The oldest describes the population you are failing. Alert on the second.

Make the wait visible. To the operator through instrumentation, and to the participant through whatever channel exists. Visible waiting is tolerable waiting, and visible ordering is defensible ordering.

Give every preference an expiry. Any rule of the form "prefer X" must be paired with a rule of the form "unless Y has waited too long." The pairing is what separates an optimization from a starvation bug. The expiry should be a number in a configuration file, with a comment explaining what trade it encodes.

Assume the switching cost is larger than you think, and measure it rather than assuming. The explicit cost is small and the implicit cost, in lost state and cold caches, is often an order of magnitude larger. A scheduler tuned on the explicit cost alone will alternate far too eagerly.

Design for partial results. The ability to return something useful when the deadline arrives beats the ability to return everything eventually, and it must be built in from the beginning because it cannot be added at the end.

Finally, remember that the queue is a shock absorber and not a storage device. Its purpose is to smooth brief mismatches between arrival and service, not to hold a permanent backlog. A queue whose length has a nonzero long run average is not absorbing shocks. It is failing to keep up, and every item in it is paying for a capacity decision that someone declined to make. No amount of clever ordering rescues a system whose arrival rate exceeds its service rate. Ordering decides who suffers. Only capacity decides whether anyone does.


XII. THE ANATOMY OF A SWAP

Consider a resource that can hold only one tenant at a time, where installing a tenant is slow and evicting one is fast, and where the population of would be tenants is larger than one. This arrangement is common enough to deserve its own analysis, and it behaves differently from the ordinary queue in ways that catch people out.

The first difference is that the cost of a decision is not symmetric. Admitting a request from the resident tenant is nearly free. Admitting a request from anyone else costs an eviction plus an installation, during which the resource serves nobody. The scheduler is therefore choosing not between orderings but between an ordering and a tax, and the tax is large enough that it distorts every other consideration.

The second difference is that the tax is paid by the wrong party. The request that triggers the swap waits for the installation. The requests behind it wait too, including requests from the tenant that was just evicted, who now must wait for their own reinstallation. A rapid alternation between two tenants can reach a state where the resource does nothing but install, serve one request, evict, install, serve one request, and so on, converting almost all of its capacity into overhead. This is thrashing again, in its purest form, and it arrives suddenly rather than gradually: the system is fine while one tenant dominates and collapses when demand splits evenly.

The natural defense is hysteresis. Once a tenant is installed, keep it for at least some minimum period, or until at least some minimum amount of work has been served, regardless of what else is waiting. Hysteresis converts a system that can thrash into one that cannot, at the price of a guaranteed worst case wait for everyone who is not resident. That price is the right one to pay, and the fact that it is a guaranteed cost rather than a probabilistic one makes it easier to reason about, though not easier to accept.

The third difference is the one that traps the unwary. If the scheduler prefers the resident tenant, and if requests for the resident tenant keep arriving, the non resident tenants are not merely delayed. They are excluded indefinitely, and the exclusion is invisible in every completion based metric. The resource will report high utilization, high throughput, and excellent latency, because every request it completed was a cheap one. The expensive requests are still in the queue, aging, and the only instrument that can see them is the one that measures the age of the oldest waiter.

Preference for the resident is nonetheless correct as a default, because the alternative, treating all tenants alike, means paying the installation tax on a large fraction of requests. The resolution is the same hybrid as before: prefer the resident, but run a clock on everyone else, and when the clock expires, swap regardless of how much cheap work is available. The clock is the whole policy. Set it too long and the excluded population experiences the system as broken. Set it too short and the resource thrashes. The correct value is a function of the installation cost and the tolerable wait, and since both vary by tenant, a single global clock is a compromise that suits nobody exactly and everybody adequately.

A subtlety arises when the tenants are not equally expensive to install. A small tenant installs quickly and a large one slowly, which means the tax for switching to the large tenant is higher, which means a greedy scheduler will postpone it more often, which means the large tenant is starved more severely than the small ones. The policy that looks neutral is in fact biased against the expensive, and the bias compounds: the longer the large tenant waits, the more cheap work accumulates in front of it. Correcting this requires weighting the aging by the expected cost, so that expensive tenants age faster, which is exactly the opposite of what a naive efficiency argument would suggest.

There is one more property of swappable resources that is easy to miss: the eviction destroys accumulated memory. Whatever the resident learned while serving, whatever state it built up, is gone when it is displaced, and reinstallation restores the tenant but not the learning. A system that swaps frequently therefore loses not only the installation time but also all of the efficiency that would have come from warm state. The true cost of a swap is the installation plus the discarded memory plus the slow rebuilding of that memory afterwards, and the last term is the one that never appears in anybody's estimate.


XIII. THE ECONOMICS OF IDLE

There is a persistent belief among people who buy capacity that idle capacity is waste, and it is responsible for an extraordinary amount of misery.

The belief has an obvious appeal. A machine sitting idle produced nothing and cost something, and if the load could be packed more densely, fewer machines would be needed and the same work would get done. The accounting is correct as far as it goes. What it omits is that the idle capacity was not doing nothing. It was absorbing variance, and variance absorption is a service with a value, even though it appears on no invoice.

Run the arithmetic again. A resource at fifty percent utilization has short queues and comfortable response times, and half its capacity appears to be wasted. Raise it to ninety percent and you have nearly doubled the useful output of the same hardware, and the queue has grown by far more than double, and the tail has grown by more than the queue. At ninety five percent the system is one bad minute away from a backlog it cannot clear. The headroom was not waste. It was the reason the system worked.

The same mistake appears in human systems and is more damaging there, because people do not degrade gracefully. A hospital running its operating theaters at ninety five percent occupancy has no capacity for the unexpected, and the unexpected is the entire point of a hospital. A team scheduled to ninety five percent of its available hours cannot absorb a sick day, and every interruption becomes a deadline slip. The buffer that looked like slack was the mechanism by which the system recovered from the ordinary noise of existence.

There is a related error, which is to measure utilization over too long a window. A resource that is at forty percent utilization averaged over an hour may have been saturated for six minutes and idle for the rest, and the people who arrived during those six minutes experienced a completely different system from the one the dashboard describes. Averaging hides bursts, and bursts are where the queue forms. The useful measurement is the distribution of utilization over short windows, not the mean over long ones.

None of this is an argument for unlimited headroom. Capacity costs money, and there is a level of reserve beyond which the insurance costs more than it returns. The argument is only that the correct level is not zero, that it must be chosen deliberately, and that choosing it requires knowing the shape of the demand rather than only its total. The operator who wants a number can start from this: the more variable the arrivals and the more variable the service times, the more headroom is required for the same experience, and the more the system is built by composing many parts, the more the tail matters and therefore the more headroom each part requires.

The final irony is that the pressure to eliminate idle capacity is strongest exactly when it should be weakest. When budgets are tight, the headroom looks like an easy saving, and cutting it produces no immediate consequence, because the consequence is probabilistic and arrives later, during an event that will be blamed on the event rather than on the decision. The cut is rewarded in the quarter it is made and punished in a quarter nobody connects to it. Queueing behavior is slow to teach its lessons and organizations are quick to forget them, which is why the same mistake is made repeatedly by people who are individually intelligent and collectively amnesiac.


XIV. THE ARITHMETIC OF BATCHING

One more arrangement deserves attention, because it inverts several of the rules established so far. Some resources serve many participants at once more cheaply than they serve them one at a time, and where that is true, almost everything changes.

The classic example is transport. A bus carrying forty passengers does not cost forty times what a bus carrying one costs. The driver, the fuel, the route, and the schedule are shared, and the marginal cost of an additional passenger is nearly nothing until the bus is full. The consequence is that the operator wants passengers to arrive together, and the passengers want to be served immediately, and the timetable is the negotiated settlement between those wishes.

Many computational resources have this property in a more extreme form. The expense is not in doing the work but in fetching what the work needs, and once fetched, applying it to one item or to thirty costs nearly the same. Under those conditions, serving participants in strict sequence is close to the worst possible policy, because each participant pays the full fetch cost alone. Gathering them into a group and serving the group as a unit divides that cost among everyone in it.

This produces a genuinely different optimization. In an ordinary queue, waiting is pure loss: the time a request spends waiting is subtracted from nobody's benefit and added to somebody's cost. In a batching system, a small amount of waiting is productive, because it allows a larger group to form, and a larger group is cheaper per member. The optimal policy is therefore not to serve immediately but to wait briefly, and the question becomes how briefly.

Two forces bound the answer. Waiting longer gathers more participants, which lowers the cost per participant, with diminishing returns as the group approaches whatever size the resource can handle at once. Waiting longer also directly increases everyone's latency, with no diminishing returns whatsoever: the second unit of waiting hurts exactly as much as the first. The optimum sits where the marginal saving from a larger group stops exceeding the marginal cost of the extra delay, and since the saving diminishes and the cost does not, an optimum always exists and is usually reached sooner than intuition suggests.

There is a trap here that catches many implementations. Under light load, the group never fills, so a policy that waits for a full group waits for the timeout every time, and light load produces the worst latency in the system. The counterintuitive result is that such a system feels slowest when it is least busy, which users find baffling and which looks like a bug in every profile. The correct policy adapts: serve immediately when nobody else is waiting, and gather only when there is something to gather. Expressed differently, the batch should form from the backlog that already exists, not from a hope about arrivals that have not happened.

Continuous batching pushes this further by abandoning the idea of a group with a beginning and an end. Instead, participants join and leave an ongoing stream of work, so that a newcomer does not wait for the current group to finish, and a finisher does not hold its slot until its neighbors are done. This removes the worst property of fixed grouping, which is that the group runs at the speed of its slowest member and releases nobody until all are complete. It also removes the clean accounting of the batch, and it complicates every measurement, because a participant's experience now depends on who else happened to be in flight during its lifetime rather than on any property of its own.

The interaction between batching and the tail deserves a note. Batching improves throughput and usually improves the median, and its effect on the tail depends entirely on whether group membership is coupled. If one slow member delays the whole group, batching converts a single slow event into many slow experiences, and the tail gets dramatically worse even as the average improves. If members are independent within the group, batching leaves the tail roughly where it was. This distinction is the single most important question to ask about any batching implementation, and it is asked far too rarely.

Finally, batching interacts with the memory discussed earlier in a way that is either a gift or a trap. If the members of a group share the preparation, then grouping participants who need the same preparation is enormously cheaper than grouping strangers. The scheduler now has a reason to prefer certain combinations, which reintroduces the placement problem, and with it the possibility that some participant is never conveniently groupable and therefore never served. The remedy, once again, is a clock on the oldest waiter, and by now it should be clear that this remedy is not a detail of any particular design but the universal price of every preference a scheduler is allowed to express.


XV. WHAT WAITING TEACHES

I want to close by returning to the thing itself, the experience of waiting, because it is easy to lose sight of it amid the arithmetic.

Waiting is a transfer. When a resource is scarce and demand exceeds it, somebody's time is being spent instead of somebody else's money, and the choice of whose time is a choice about value. The bank that staffs one teller at lunchtime has decided that its customers' collective hours are cheaper than an additional salary. That may be correct, and it is certainly a decision, and it is made more often by default than by deliberation. Every queue embodies an implicit exchange rate between the operator's costs and the participants' time, and most operators have never calculated that rate or even noticed that they set it.

Engineers meet this most directly in the form of the build that takes four minutes, the test suite that takes an hour, the deployment that takes a day. Each is a queue with a single participant, and each embodies the same exchange: the cost of making it faster, weighed against the cost of everyone waiting. The difference is that the waiting is borne by the same people who could fix it, which makes the accounting honest in a way that customer facing queues rarely are. It is not an accident that internal tools get faster when the people who build them also use them, or that they stagnate when those populations diverge.

The other thing waiting teaches is humility about prediction. Almost every queueing disaster begins with a forecast that demand would stay in a range, and almost every forecast is defeated by an event that nobody put in the model. The response is not better forecasting, which has diminishing returns, but better behavior at the edges: systems that degrade instead of collapsing, that refuse instead of accepting and failing, that shed the least valuable load first and say so. Robustness is not the ability to handle the load you predicted. It is the ability to behave sensibly under the load you did not.

There is an old observation that no one ever complains about a queue that moves. It is not quite true, but it is close enough to be useful, and it points at something the arithmetic cannot capture. What makes waiting intolerable is not duration but uncertainty, and not uncertainty alone but the suspicion of arbitrariness, the sense that the order is not what it claims to be and that patience is not being rewarded. A system that is slow but transparently ordered retains its legitimacy. A system that is fast but visibly capricious does not. This is as true of a compiler as it is of a customs hall.

The engineer's job, then, is not only to make the resource faster, although that helps, and not only to make the ordering optimal, although the arithmetic repays study. It is to make the arrangement one that a reasonable participant, told how it works, would accept as reasonable. That is a design constraint like any other, and it is the one most often omitted from the specification, and it is the one that determines whether the thing you built will be trusted.

Everything else is scheduling.
